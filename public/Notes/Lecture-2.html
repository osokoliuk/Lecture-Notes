Definition 2(Definition).

Let s:I↦R be defined by s(t)=∫t0​t​∣α′(u)∣du=Lt0​t​(α). The function u↦∣α′(u)∣ is in general just continuous, not necessarily differentiable or C∞(I). Hence, s in general is just C1(I), as due to the fundamental theorem of calculus, s′(t)=∣α′(t)∣. In particular, s is not a diffeomorphism, unless ∣α(t)∣=0∀t∈I. In that specific scenario, s is a diffeomorphism between I and s(I). Such curves are called regular. This leads to the fact that s is C∞(I). Call J=s(I) and ϕ=s−1 an inverse of s such that ϕ∘s=idI​. Now, define β(s)=α∘ϕ:J↦R3. Then, using the chain rule,

β′(s)=α′(ϕ(s))ϕ′(s)=∣α′(ϕ(s))∣α′(ϕ(s))​⟹∣β(s)∣=1∀s∈J

Such that the “velocity” of the curve β is constant at any point in time.

Definition 3(Definition).

α:I↦R3 is parameterised by arc length (p.a.l.) if ∣α′(t)∣=1∀t∈I.

Theorem 4(Theorem).

Every regular curve can be parameterised by an arc length.

Remark 7(Remark).

Let T(s)=α′(s) where α is p.a.l. and ∣T(s)∣=1. It is a trivial fact that ⟨T(s),T(s)⟩=0. As the inner product is linear, taking a derivative with respect to s leads to

⟨T′(s),T(s)⟩+⟨T(s),T′(s)⟩=0⟹2⟨T(s),T′(s)⟩=0⟹⟨T(s),T′(s)⟩=0

where we have used the fact that the inner product is symmetric. Hence, ∀s∈J, T′(s) is orthogonal to T(s).

Definition 8(Frenet' Formula).

N(s)=∣T′(s)∣T′(s)​=k(s)T′(s)​

is the normal vector to α at a point s∈J and k(s) is the curvature of α at s∈J. Moreover,

B(s)=T(s)∧N(s)

is the binormal vector to α at a point s∈J. Then, {B(s),T(s),N(s)} form an orthonormal basis on R3, usually called a Frenet’ basis with each element being C∞(J). Note that,

T′(s)=k(s)N(s)B′(s)=τ(s)N(s)N′(s)=−k(s)T(s)−τ(s)B(s)

where τ(s) is the torsion of α at s∈J. It is simply τ(s)=⟨B′(s),N(s)⟩, i.e. a projection of B′(s) onto N(s).

Theorem 10(Theorem).

Let α:I↦R3, p.a.l. with k>0. Then, α is planar iff τ=0.

Proof.

Suppose that τ=0. Then, ∣B′(t)∣=0, so B(t)=u, some constant vector. Then,

⟨α(t),u⟩′=⟨α′(t),u⟩+⟨α(t),0⟩=⟨v,u⟩+⟨α(t),0⟩=0

using the fact that B(t) is orthogonal to α at any point in time. Then, ⟨α(t),u⟩=a with a∈R. But that just means that α(t) projected onto z-axis for any t∈I is constant, hence α(t) is a regular curve.□

Theorem 11(Theorem).

α:I↦R3 p.a.l. with k=0 iff α is a segment of a line.

Proof.

⟸​ Trivial.
⟹​ Now assume that k=0, then T′(t)=0 hence T(t) is a constant. But T(t)=α′(t) so if T(t) is constant for any t∈I then α′(t)=v for some constant vector. Integrating, we get α(t)=tv+v0​ where v0​ is the constant of integration. This is an equation for a line.□
